What to Know Before Watching F1

Understand the sport beyond the chequered flag. Learn the basics of Formula 1 and follow every race with a better understanding.

Understand the Major Flags

Flags communicate important information to drivers. Hover over a flag to learn what it means.

Checkered Flag

The checkered flag signals the end of a session or race. In a Grand Prix, it is shown to the leading driver as they cross the finish line, followed by the other drivers.

Remember: The checkered flag signals the finish, not necessarily who won.

Blue Flag

A blue flag warns a driver that a faster car is approaching and is about to lap them. The driver being lapped must allow the faster car to pass according to the applicable rules.

Remember: Blue means a faster car is coming through.

Yellow Flag

A yellow flag warns drivers of danger on or near the track, such as a crashed car or debris. Drivers must slow down, avoid overtaking under the applicable restrictions, and be prepared to change direction or stop.

Remember: Yellow means danger. Slow down and stay alert.

Red Flag

A red flag signals that a session has been suspended, usually because of a serious incident or unsafe track conditions. Drivers must slow down and follow the race control instructions, usually returning to the pit lane.

Remember: Red means the session has been stopped.

A Week of Pure Chaos

Seven days of anticipation. Three days of on-track action. One driver standing on the top step.

Hover over a day to explore the race week.

The Build-up

Teams analyse previous races, prepare strategies and begin final preparations for the upcoming Grand Prix.

Team Preparation

Engineers study data, review car performance and plan possible race strategies.

The Countdown

Teams refine their plans, while fans and drivers build anticipation for the weekend.

Media & Preparation

Drivers may attend media sessions, while teams complete preparations at the circuit.

Practice Day

Practice sessions help drivers learn the track, test setups and find the right balance. Sprint weekends use a different schedule.

Qualifying & Sprint

Qualifying determines the starting order for the Grand Prix. At Sprint events, the Sprint race adds another competitive session.

Grand Prix Day

The main event. Drivers race over a set distance, manage tyres, make pit stops and battle for championship points.

The winner usually earns 25 points. The top ten finishers score points.

The exact schedule varies by Grand Prix. Sprint weekends change the usual Friday and Saturday sessions.

VSC vs SC

Two ways to control the race when danger appears on track.

Virtual Safety Car (VSC)

Drivers must slow down and follow a required pace without overtaking. The Safety Car does not physically lead the cars around the track.

Safety Car (SC)

A physical Safety Car enters the track and leads the drivers in a controlled line. Drivers must follow it and cannot race freely until it returns to the pits.
